# Stronger Workshop Ideas

Nov 23, 2026 · @phoneix

My pick is Kickoff Lab: first-years practise running a team's first meeting twice in one hour, which gives you a before-and-after measure built into the session. Your team's idea wasn't in the PDF, so add it to the ranking sheet and I'll score it against these three.

## 1. Kickoff Lab: run your team's first meeting

Scores 96/100 on the rubric. The problems that sink teams in week 8 (someone goes silent, one person does everything, deadlines slip) usually start with expectations nobody set in week 1. Participants run a kickoff twice, badly then well, and leave with a charter template.

- **Audience:** first-year EAIT students about to start their first team-project course. ENGG1100 is built around team design projects and DECO1800 has a team project, so the need is concrete. Run it in O-Week or weeks 1 to 2.
- **Skill:** running a kickoff meeting that ends in a written team charter.
- **Inner skills:** empathy (reading teammates' real constraints) and emotional awareness (naming tension before it becomes conflict).

**SMART objectives**

1. By the end of the 60 minutes, each group writes a charter covering at least 8 of 10 checklist items: roles, availability, comms channel, response time, decision rule, conflict step, definition of done, internal deadlines, meeting rhythm, and what happens if someone goes quiet.
2. By the end of the session, each participant can name 2 hidden constraints a teammate revealed and how the charter handles them.
3. Within 4 weeks, at least half of participants report that their real team used a charter.

**The hour**

1. **0 to 5 min, hook:** anonymous live poll, "your worst group project moment".
2. **5 to 20 min, round 1:** groups of 5 get a mock project brief and secret role cards, then have 12 minutes to run a kickoff and write a charter. No guidance.
3. **20 to 30 min, reveal:** everyone reads their role card aloud; groups score their own charter against the 10-item checklist.
4. **30 to 38 min, teach:** the 5 kickoff questions. What does done look like? Who does what? When can everyone actually work? How do we talk? What happens when it goes wrong?
5. **38 to 53 min, round 2:** new brief, new role cards, same 12 minutes, now with the template. Score again.
6. **53 to 60 min, reflect:** compare round 1 and round 2 scores, fill in the exit card, take the template home.

Example role cards: "You work 20 hours a week and can only meet Tuesdays." "You want a 7 and plan to do it all yourself." "You won't speak unless someone asks you directly." "You're already in two other group projects."

**Measuring success:** checklist score in round 1 vs round 2 (measured in the room), confidence 1 to 5 before and after on the exit card, and a 4-week follow-up form.

**Cost and feasibility:** printed cards and checklists, roughly $20; optional snacks, roughly $60 for 30 people (my estimates). Run by EAIT Ambassadors in a booked tutorial room. Before the pitch, check whether ENGG1100 already runs its own team process (an older UQ page mentions one called PETS), and position yours as practice that complements it.

**Why it scores:** the two rounds give you a built-in before and after, every judge has a group project horror story, and you can hand a judge a role card mid-pitch.

## 2. Say It Plainly: explain your tech to anyone

Scores 92/100, and it's the most literal fit to "Leading out Loud": slide 1 says technical knowledge is useless if you can't communicate it. Participants explain their own project to someone outside their field until that person can repeat it back.

- **Audience:** 2nd and 3rd year EAIT students heading into internship interviews ("tell me about a project"), hackathon pitches and capstone presentations.
- **Skill:** explaining a technical project to a non-specialist in 60 seconds.
- **Inner skills:** empathy (seeing it from the listener's side) and self mastery (staying calm when put on the spot).

**SMART objectives**

1. By the end of the session, each participant gives a 60-second explanation of their own project that a partner from another discipline restates with all 3 key points: the problem, what it does, why it matters.
2. By the end of the session, each participant's final recording uses no more than 2 jargon terms, down from their first.

**The hour**

1. **0 to 5 min, hook:** the same concept explained twice, once in jargon and once plainly. Which person would you hire?
2. **5 to 15 min, baseline:** pair up across disciplines. Each person records a 60-second explanation on their phone; the partner restates it and scores it 0 to 3.
3. **15 to 25 min, teach:** a 3-part frame (problem, thing, why it matters), the "so what?" test, and picking an analogy.
4. **25 to 45 min, telephone:** in groups of 3, A explains to B, B explains to C, C writes one sentence on what it is. Compare with what A meant. Round 2 adds banned-word cards: 3 jargon terms from your field you can't use.
5. **45 to 55 min, re-record:** new 60-second recording, partner restates and scores again.
6. **55 to 60 min, reflect:** play back the first and last recordings, fill in the exit card.

**Measuring success:** restatement score (0 to 3) and jargon count, first recording vs last, plus confidence 1 to 5 before and after.

**Cost and feasibility:** close to free. Phones for recording, printed cards, one room. Mixed-discipline pairing is easy because EAIT already spans engineering, computing, design and architecture.

**Why it's second:** the measure relies on peer scoring, which runs generous, and "communication" sounds broad until you explain the restatement test. The pitch move is strong though: explain your workshop to a judge, then ask them to say it back.

## 3. Review Without the Ego: give technical feedback

Scores 80/100. Team projects stall when feedback either never gets said or lands as an attack. Participants practise writing and receiving critique on a real piece of work.

- **Audience:** 2nd year students starting team software or design projects.
- **Skill:** writing specific, actionable, respectful technical feedback, and receiving it without getting defensive.
- **Inner skills:** emotional awareness and self mastery.

**SMART objectives**

1. By the end of the session, each participant rewrites 3 harsh review comments so each scores at least 4 out of 6 on a rubric (specific, actionable, respectful, 2 points each).
2. By the end of the session, each pair finds at least 2 of the 4 issues planted in a second piece of work.

**The hour**

1. **0 to 5 min, hook:** a realistic harsh review comment on screen. How would you react?
2. **5 to 20 min, round 1:** pairs review a short pull request (or a CAD drawing) with planted issues and write comments.
3. **20 to 30 min, swap:** the author rates how each comment felt and whether they'd act on it.
4. **30 to 38 min, teach:** observation, impact, suggestion; and labelling comments as blocking, suggestion or nitpick.
5. **38 to 53 min, round 2:** a new piece of work, plus replying to one critical comment on your own.
6. **53 to 60 min, score and reflect.**

**Measuring success:** rubric score round 1 vs round 2, and the number of planted issues found.

**Cost and feasibility:** free tools (GitHub) and laptops, but you have to prepare the planted-issue artefacts in advance.

**Why it ranks lower:** the audience is narrower, prep is heavier, and judges outside computing need more setup to feel the problem. Running a code track and a design/CAD track on the same method fixes most of that.

## Moves that lift any idea, including yours

The single biggest edge is piloting it: "we ran it with 6 people and checklist scores went from 4 to 8" beats any assumption on a slide.

- **Pilot 20 minutes of it** with friends or your residential college before the pitch, and put the before-and-after number on the measurement slide.
- **Let the judges do 30 seconds of the activity.** It covers Engagement and Communication at the same time.
- **Show the measuring tool itself** (the checklist, the rubric, the exit card) on a slide, not a description of it.
- **Put a cost table on the feasibility slide**, with who runs it, which room, and which week of semester.
- **Name one inner skill** (resilience, empathy, emotional awareness, self mastery) and point to the minute in the hour where it gets practised, so the theme isn't just a word on the title slide.
- **Say what you rejected and why.** Planning and Reflection explicitly rewards justified decisions, e.g. "we chose first-years over third-years because...".
- **Rehearse to 6:30**, so you have 30 seconds of buffer inside the 7-minute limit.
